# Presenting Your Work, and What Comes After

This article is for your own benefit - there's nothing to submit. Some of it touches your Course Assignment (the portfolio site, the GitHub profile), but where it does, this article is about what happens *around* or *after* the CA, not what the CA submission itself must contain. Check the Course Assignment Brief for the actual requirements, structure, and grading criteria of your CA portfolio.

## 1. Your portfolio doesn't end at the Course Assignment

The portfolio you build for your CA has a specific, fixed shape: a home page with teaser cards, three projects, a dedicated article page per project, particular fields on each. That structure exists because it's what's being graded, not because it's the only way to present a portfolio.

Once the CA is submitted, nothing stops you from continuing to develop it, or from building something different entirely, once you're not working inside those constraints. A few directions people take this:

- **Keep evolving the same site.** Add projects as you build them after graduation, swap the teaser-card format for something closer to a blog or case-study layout, drop projects that no longer represent your best work.
- **Rewrite the write-ups as case studies rather than teasers.** A teaser is built to be scanned quickly; a case study can go deeper into the problem, your specific decisions, and what you'd do differently now.
- **Start a cleaner site later, once you have stronger material.** There's no rule that your CA portfolio has to be the one you use for job hunting a year from now. Some people treat it as a starting point rather than a finished product.

None of this is required or expected. It's just worth knowing that the fixed structure you're building right now is a course requirement, not a career ceiling.

## 2. Your GitHub profile README

Your CA requires you to update your personal GitHub profile README - but doesn't prescribe exactly what it should contain the way the project README template does. That makes it worth a closer look here.

A profile README is the page someone sees when they click your username, before they've looked at any of your repositories. For someone hiring, it's often the first real impression of you as a developer.

### Worked example: before and after

**Before (technically complete, says nothing):**
```markdown
# Hi, I'm Kari

I am a frontend developer. I like coding.

## Skills
- HTML
- CSS
- JavaScript
```

**After (same person, actually useful to a reader):**
```markdown
# Kari Nordmann

Frontend developer, fagskole-educated (120 studiepoeng), based in Bergen.
I build accessible, responsive interfaces and I'm comfortable working
across the stack from plain HTML/CSS/JS through to React.

## A couple of things I've built
- **[Portfolio site](link)** - my main project site, built with React
- **[Project name](link)** - short note on what makes this one worth a look

## Currently
Looking for my first junior frontend role. Open to hearing from teams
in Bergen or remote-friendly companies in Norway.

## Get in touch
[LinkedIn](link) . [Email](mailto:...)
```

The difference isn't length, it's specificity: the second version names the qualification correctly (same point as in the previous Friday Knowledge Share), points at real things to look at, and tells the reader what you're looking for right now, which makes it easier for someone to know why they're reading it.

## 3. A pitch script for talking about your work

Your CA requires a structured 5-minute video presentation with a specific content list - that stays exactly as the brief describes it, and this isn't a substitute for it.

What's missing from most students' toolkit is a much shorter, informal version: the answer to "so what have you been working on?" at a meetup, in a first-round interview, or messaging someone on LinkedIn. Thirty seconds, not five minutes.

### Fill-in-the-blank template

> "I'm a frontend developer, recently finished at Noroff. My main project is [project name] - it's a [one-line description of what it does]. The interesting part was [one specific decision, problem, or challenge], and I ended up [what you did about it]. I'm currently [looking for my first role / interested in X kind of work]."

Worked example filled in:

> "I'm a frontend developer, recently finished at Noroff. My main project is a recipe-sharing site - it lets users save and tag their own recipes. The interesting part was making the search filter fast without a backend, so I ended up building a simple client-side index instead of filtering the full list on every keystroke. I'm currently looking for my first junior role, ideally somewhere I can keep working with React."

Notice it's built from the same material as your CV line and your GitHub README - one real project, one specific decision, stated plainly. Once you have that thirty-second version memorised, the five-minute CA video and a full interview answer both become easier, because you're just expanding the same core story rather than starting from nothing.

## 4. LinkedIn basics

You don't need a polished LinkedIn presence to get your first job, but a bare or outdated one can quietly work against you if someone looks you up after an application.

A few things worth checking, each with almost no time cost:

- **Headline** - defaults to your current or most recent role/education, but you can and should override it. "Frontend Developer | Fagskole-educated | React & JavaScript" tells someone more in five seconds than "Student at Noroff" does.
- **About section** - the same 30-second pitch from section 3 works here almost unchanged.
- **Featured section** - link your portfolio site and GitHub profile directly, so they're visible without someone having to dig.
- **Connect with people you actually meet** - classmates, teachers, anyone from a Noroff event or a meetup. A short note with the connection request ("Good talking to you at [event]") makes it far more likely to be accepted and remembered.

### Worked example: headline before and after

**Before:** `Student at Noroff`

**After:** `Frontend Developer | Fagskole-educated | React, JavaScript, Accessibility`

## 5. Staying current after graduation

You won't have a syllabus telling you what to learn next once the course ends. That's freeing, but it can also mean drifting for a while without a clear direction. A short list of low-effort ways people stay current, pick whichever fits rather than doing all of them:

- **Follow a small number of sources, not everything.** One newsletter, one podcast, one person whose blog you actually read, is more sustainable than trying to keep up with the whole industry.
- **MDN Web Docs** stays the most reliable reference for HTML, CSS and JavaScript fundamentals, and is worth returning to even after you know the basics - it gets updated as the web platform changes.
- **Revisit fundamentals occasionally, not just new frameworks.** It's tempting to chase whatever framework is trending; the underlying HTML/CSS/JS/accessibility fundamentals age far more slowly and are usually the better use of limited learning time.
- **Build small things on purpose, not just at work.** A short, finished side project teaches you more than an unfinished ambitious one - the "finished" part is the useful bit.
- **It's fine to go quiet on learning for a while after a big push (like this course).** Burnout from trying to keep learning at course-pace indefinitely is a common way people stop enjoying the field. Pace yourself.
